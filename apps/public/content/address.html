Before I say one word of policy, let me say a word about this country. America is the most successful act of nation-building in the history of the human race. We declared, against the mightiest empire on earth, that all men are created equal, and then we defeated that empire in the field. We planted a republic of laws and grew it across a continent. We fought our bloodiest war to end slavery, and hundreds of thousands of Americans went to their graves to finish it.

We harnessed electricity. We taught the world to fly. We broke fascism, outlasted communism, conquered polio, split the atom to power our cities, built the internet, and left our footprints on the Moon. And when our harvests came in, we fed nations that could not feed themselves. We were gracious to the beleaguered peoples who emerged from the wreckage of the failed communist nations that imploded under their own false theories and corruption.

Our commitment to liberty and fundamental individual rights has made America a magnet for the productive geniuses of the Earth who leave behind their entire lives just for the chance to be an American. America is a miracle for the boldest men and women on the face of the earth – the creative masters of arts, the revealers of new knowledge through science, the developers of astonishing, almost unbelievable technologies, the independently minded and the first principles thinkers – all find sanctuary, opportunity and grace in this nation.

We channel our brilliance into ideas, goods and services that enrich the entire world, radiating virtue and spreading our values without even having to try, merely by our unmatched example of the great achievements and flourishing that our values, put into action, naturally produce. That is two and a half centuries of the American record, and no honest man or woman can survey it and call this country anything but a marvel. America is a blessing to the world.

The Christian values that guided the hand of our forefathers imbued the nation with fundamental human dignity, religious tolerance, and the necessity of public virtue. We’ve woven these values into our Constitution from the beginning. The values of the revolution — Christian values, Enlightenment values, and our inheritance of the ancient Greek and Roman virtues and ways of government — these are the root values of American civilization that we can trace back through the ages and to which we can attribute all of our success. And those roots run deeper still.

Go back before Athens — before the Parthenon rose, before the first assembly met — and you find the peoples who would one day build Athens and Rome living under the most total authority the West has ever known. A great historian, Fustel de Coulanges, reconstructed that world from the rites and laws the ancients themselves preserved: every family its own church; the hearth fire a god; the father its priest; every soul the property of the blood and of the dead. No one belonged to himself. And the story of Western civilization is the story of how, revolution by revolution, human beings walked out of that world.

The first revolution took the scepter from the priest. The ancient king was ruler and holy man fused in one person, and to resist him was sacrilege — until the great families rose, took his rule, and left him his altar. From that day forward, power over men was one thing, and divine authority another. Every time we say that no office makes its holder sacred — that power is a trust and not an anointing — we live in the wake of that first revolution.

The second happened inside the house. The ancient clan was a nation in miniature: one master, absolute, and every son and servant bound beneath him for life — until the clan broke apart, and the individual stepped out from behind the patriarch. Every time we insist that rights belong to persons, not to bloodlines, we are heirs of the second.

The third stormed the gates. Outside the sacred families lived a multitude with no holy fire and no honored tomb — in the city, but never of it — until they forced the question. In Rome they simply walked out, to a hill beyond the walls, and the city discovered it could not live without them. They won tribunes. They won law written down and posted where every eye could check it. They won their place. Every charter that begins "We the People," and not "we the founding families," is the third revolution still speaking.

The fourth changed the question that government answers. For ages the city asked what the deified ancestors required. Now it asked what the civic good required. Law became a thing citizens deliberate; office became a thing citizens confer and can recall. Every ballot cast in this country is the fourth revolution in your hand.

And the fifth arrived not by sword or senate but in a sentence: render unto Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and unto God the things that are God's. Faith and state, one flesh since before memory, were cut apart, and a territory was declared inside every human being where no government may enter. Conscience became a fact that law would one day have to honor. Our First Amendment descends from that sentence.

The march that began with the barons at Runnymede, who wrested Magna Carta from a king, culminated in the American Revolution — the last revolution our would ever need to win by force of arms, for our nation carries within it the means of change by argument, reason, and agreement. Our liberty we inherited in steps, revolution by revolution, each one enlarging who counts and shrinking what those in power may take from an individual or claim over the person.

Every revolution in liberty's long march returned to human beings something power had taken from them. The Garden is that march, taking its next step.

Our fate depends on our adherence to these, our root values, from which all our strength and success are drawn.

It is precisely because our successes are so many that we can afford honesty about our few failures. Weak nations hide their wounds. Strong nations heal them. And healing our own wounds is itself an old American success: we amended our Constitution, we enlarged the franchise, we tore segregation out of our laws, and each time the house stood taller for it.

So hear what follows as it is meant. This land was built by hard work, and it was built by all of us. But one family of Americans was written out of the will. Denied the wealth their labor created. Denied a national inheritance of their own. Denied even the names of their ancestors. The policy I present today, called The Garden, does not pull one brick from the house we raised together. It fortifies the house, by welcoming into the inheritance the Americans who were shut out of it. A country that can admit its wrong, and right it, without taking a single thing from anyone, is not a country in decline. It is a country still worthy of the words it was born declaring.

In 1526 — five hundred years ago, almost to the day — a king wrote letters of protest that survive to this hour.

His name was Afonso. He ruled the Kingdom of Kongo, a nation that sent ambassadors to Portugal and corresponded with Rome, a nation of courts, of records, of law. And it was being robbed of its people. Afonso watched his people marched to the coast and taken by the ships, and he wrote to the King of Portugal as one head of state writes to another, demanding that the taking end.

Those letters exist. Scholars read them now, five centuries later: the testimony of a wronged nation, in its own hand, given while the wrong was still being done.

We begin here because these letters refute the oldest falsehood in the American story — the falsehood that the men and women brought to this country in chains came from nowhere, belonged to nothing, and carried no law. They came from nations. They were citizens. And the nations they came from had names.

Slavery was two crimes. America has named only one of them.

The first crime was against persons. Human beings were stolen, worked without pay, and sold. Families were broken at the auction block. Names, languages, and faiths were stripped away by design. Children inherited the theft. This crime we have named. We teach it, we mourn it, and rightly so.

The second crime was against nations, and it has never been named at all.

Every enslaved person was a citizen of a real polity. Some of these were great states: the Oyo Empire. Kongo. Ndongo. Some were kingdoms of long diplomatic reach: Allada sent an ambassador to the court of Louis XIV in 1670. Some were city-states destroyed outright: Owu was razed in the 1820s, and its people were sold. And some, in a way that should matter deeply to every American, were republics with no king at all — village nations governed by open assembly, by councils of elders, by the ordered voice of the governed.

We know these nations by their own evidence. Venture Smith's narrative, published in 1798, preserves nearly all we know of Dukandarra, his homeland. Ayuba Suleiman Diallo, a citizen of Bundu, saw his biography published in London in 1734. Omar ibn Said of Futa Toro wrote his autobiography in Arabic in 1831, and the manuscript rests today in the Library of Congress. The Mende men of the Amistad named their home polities one by one in an American courtroom, and in 1841 the Supreme Court of the United States heard a former President of the United States argue their cause, and ruled them free, because the record proved they had been born free citizens of their own nations.

The slave trade did not merely take these citizens. It dissolved their citizenships, and it did so deliberately. Captives were mixed on the ships and mixed again on the plantations so that no group could plan in its own language. Polities were scattered until no assembly of their members could ever be called again. Then came the final act of the crime: the descendants were taught that their ancestors had come from no nation and carried no law.

That second crime has a remedy. The remedy is the subject of this address.

Consider the Igbo, likely the largest single source of the enslaved in Virginia and Maryland. They carried a saying across the centuries: Igbo enwe eze. The Igbo have no king. In their homeland they governed themselves by open assembly, by councils of elders, by age-grades that gave every generation its voice. Authority sat in gatherings of the governed. The consent of the governed was not just theory. It was their daily practice, for generations, until capture destroyed their citizenship and carried them across the sea.

Slavery operated in the American colonies for more than a hundred and fifty years before 1776. So when the founders proclaimed to the world that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed, there were already Igbo men and women in bondage a day's ride from Philadelphia who had governed themselves by that principle in their own nations, before those nations were taken from them. Self-rule was not a European invention handed to peoples who had never known it. It was discovered again and again across human history—by Greeks and Romans, and by African nations the slave trade erased. The Igbo kept councils and answered to no king. The men and women in those fields were no blank slate on which liberty had to be written; they arrived already carrying it, and had it stripped away. The nation that wrote those words owes a debt to people who knew self-government before they were ever brought here. Here is how we pay it.

The whole of this policy fits in a single sentence: The Garden returns, to the people this nation wronged most, the liberties our government long ago took from everyone — at no cost and no loss to anyone — by participating states offering limited, conditional monopolies, in select industries and select districts, to nations the descendants themselves will found anew.

Every word of that sentence is precise. Everything that follows is that sentence, unfolded.

Any true remedy for a wrong of this size must pass two tests.

The first test is voice. A remedy must reach the wronged in a form they govern themselves, at a scale where each person can be heard. A cash transfer to forty million people fails this test. Nothing is built. Nothing is governed. No single voice rises above the mass, and no one can answer for what becomes of it. A remedy that vanishes rather than builds and compounds is a gesture but not a lasting solution.

The second test is harm. A remedy must take nothing from anyone else. Not a tax dollar. Not a job, a seat, a contract, or a customer. Racial preferences fail this test, because every reserved seat is taken from a particular person who earned it and wronged no one. The injury does not heal; it changes address. And resentment grows between neighbors who owe each other nothing but goodwill.

A remedy that passes both tests must give the wronged something no other citizen currently holds and no other citizen is currently competing for on merit. That sounds impossible. In fact, our government is holding a great store of it at this hour.

Our founding creed is that government does not create rights. Rights pre-exist in persons by nature; governments are instituted to secure them. Among these rights is the right to earn a living in any honest trade.

Now follow that creed one step further than we usually take it. When government prohibits an industry, the right to practice that trade does not vanish, and it does not become unowned. It is taken. Taken from every citizen at once, the majority overreaching against the minority who would otherwise have entered that trade. The American people have largely accepted these prohibitions as prudent, and this policy asks for none of them to be repealed. But every prohibition leaves the state holding something that was never its own: a liberty confiscated from all of its people.

That store of taken liberties is where restitution comes from.

When the state restores a prohibited liberty exclusively to a wronged nation, within a region that volunteers it, no citizen loses anything at that moment. Every other citizen's position is exactly what it was the day before. Their loss is real, but it is old. It occurred on the day of prohibition, equally, for everyone, and it deepens by nothing when the wronged are made whole. The state sets one taking, prohibition, against another, slavery, and pays its oldest debt out of what its own overreach left sitting in its hands. No new victim is created anywhere, because nothing is taken from anyone that was not taken long ago, from everyone alike.

Understand clearly what government does here, and what it does not do. It does not create a right; rights are not its to create. It does not grant a favor and it does not dispense generosity. It acts as a debtor making full and final restitution. The wronged are paid in liberty itself.

Under this program, the state chooses to grant the exclusive right, within its cooperating districts, for a refounded nation to enter an industry currently prohibited to every American. Call it what it is: a monopoly — regional, granted, and conditioned. America has never been against monopoly as such; America is against monopoly seized and abused. The same founders who broke from a king wrote monopoly into the Constitution itself: Article One empowers Congress to secure to inventors the exclusive right to their discoveries — a granted monopoly, limited in scope, aimed at a public purpose — and it built the most inventive nation on earth. Your electric utility is a granted monopoly. So is every patent in your pharmacy. What the law condemns is dominion seized by force and turned against the public. What this program grants is the opposite in every particular. Regional, never national, so a citizen shut out of one district opens in another. Conferred by fixed public rule, never seized. Bound to state law in every clause. And aimed at the oldest public purpose there is: the payment of a public debt. Prohibition holds investment at zero, an unnatural state imposed by law. Exclusivity gives capital the confidence to move and jump-starts an industry.

Every candidate liberty faces a single admissibility test: is this trade closed to every citizen at the moment of the grant? If yes, restoring it to a nation takes nothing from anyone. If a grant would take anything from any citizen — a seat, a contract, a customer, a place in a line, a market anyone now operates in — it is barred.

Here is what that means in practice.

Cannabis is the flagship: cultivation, processing, and retail. It is federally prohibited to every citizen, and it is the largest new legal market in a generation as the states legalize. Several states already operate reserved-license programs of their own, which shows that regulatory machinery of this kind can be built. This program stands on firmer constitutional ground than any of them, as this address will show.

Gaming and sports wagering is the proven precedent, with compact structures and decades of settled case law behind it.

Regulated therapeutic substances offer the earliest entry. Oregon, Colorado and New Mexico have made supervised psilocybin services lawful, more states are drafting, and in every state where these services remain prohibited, there are no incumbents to displace.

There is a second such medicine, nearer to hand than most Americans know. Ibogaine, a compound drawn from a shrub of the West and Central African forest and long used in the healing traditions of the peoples who first knew its power, has shown early promise against three of the cruelest afflictions we bear: the grip of opioid addiction, the wounds of post-traumatic stress, and the lasting damage of injury to the brain. And here Texas leads the nation. Texas has committed public funds to carry ibogaine through the clinical trials the federal government demands — the largest investment any state has ever made in a medicine of this kind — seeking lawful approval above all for the veterans who bore our battles and came home bearing them still.

Ibogaine is prohibited to every American today, which is the very reason it belongs on this list: to restore it to a nation takes nothing from anyone, because no citizen may lawfully hold it now. Should the law open that door, Texas might choose to open it first — and in some districts exclusively — through one of the nations this address will shortly describe, in districts the state puts forward. Nations would hold the exclusive right to distribute or administer the medicine in districts they negotiate for, bound in every particular to the statutes and safeguards the state requires — a right conferred, as everywhere in this program, by fixed rule and never by favor.

Weigh what this would mean. A medicine of Africa, drawn from the forest by the hands of African healers, restored on American soil to the heirs of Africa's stolen nations — and carried onward by them to their own countrymen in the grip of addiction, and to the soldiers still searching for the road home from war. A wronged people made the keepers of an African gift, and in the keeping made whole — not by a tax laid upon their neighbors, but by a living enterprise of healing, offered to the very nation that once held them in chains. This is restoration in its deepest sense: a wrong transfigured into a wellspring of healing; a debt paid in the one coin that costs no one — a life given back its future.

And the promise is only widening — and no longer among the states alone. Kentucky, Mississippi, Oklahoma, and Tennessee have taken up Texas's cause, Michigan now weighs it, and the federal government itself has joined them: President Trump has signed an executive order directing his administration to speed ibogaine toward lawful use — opening a pathway for suffering patients to reach it, quickening the FDA's review, and pledging federal dollars to labor beside the very states already carrying the work forward. Here is the rarest thing in American government, and the very thing this address asks for — a state and the nation moving as one, each loosing by its own hand what that same hand had bound.

And because every grant is limited in geography, perhaps to a few districts, and every state participates voluntarily, industry by industry. A citizen who wants to open a cannabis shop loses nothing when a district is granted, because the trade was already closed to everyone there — and where the trade is open, the program cannot touch it, by its own test. Every legal market in the country remains exactly as open the day after a grant as the day before.

If this still sounds like theory, look at Connecticut. Connecticut has already done it.
Connecticut wanted to right the wrongs the nation imposed on the Mohegan Tribe and the Mash Inn tuck it Pee kwat Tribal Nation. Its remedy took precisely the form I have just described. Casino gaming was prohibited to every citizen of the state. Connecticut restored that liberty exclusively to the two wronged nations, within their lands. No neighbor lost anything, because no neighbor's position changed at all.

Look at what grew. Health clinics and elder care. Full scholarships. Tribal courts and public safety. Parks and restoration. Ancestral grave sites restored. One of the largest Native American museums in the world, the Mash Inn tuck it Pee kwat Museum and Research Center. A partnership to restore the polluted Norwich State Hospital brownfields to develop the highly anticipated Preston Riverwalk. Enterprises that rank among the largest employers in their region, hiring members and non-members alike by the thousands. Billions of dollars paid, over the life of the compacts, into the treasury of the state. By this one method to right a wrong, two nations have become deeply integrated with the American republic — two pillars of the Connecticut economy — and their citizens thousands of well-respected members of the Connecticut family.

But the deepest lesson of Connecticut is not the money. It is the scale. These nations are small, a few thousand citizens each. At that scale, an elder's need can move a budget, and one family's argument can redirect a plan. The consent of the governed is not a phrase there. It is the Tuesday meeting. Small republics hear their people.

That lesson decides the shape of everything that follows.

The community this program serves is the American Descendants of Slavery: the descendants of persons enslaved in the United States. That defines who may join. It is the soil, not the structure.
The structure is African American Republican Tribal Nations: voluntary, self-governing republics, founded by descendants, each rooted in a documented historic African nation destroyed or its people taken by the slave trade. Many small nations, not one mass organization — because small republics hear their people, and because the historical truth is that there were many nations, each deserving to have its name spoken again.

Membership is chosen, never assigned. There is no blood test, and there is no state sorting of citizens by ancestry. Slavery's deliberate mixing put ancestral certainty forever out of reach, and a free country does not classify its citizens by blood in any case. Each descendant weighs whatever they hold — family memory, genetic ancestry results, historical research, conviction — and elects, from the Founding Roster of Historic Nations, the one historic nation from which they believe themselves most plausibly descended. Even the nations whose names were fully erased will be represented: their descendants may found republics named for the rivers and coasts of origin where nations are documented by the historical record, so that the nameless, too, stand again in a nation of their own.

Three founding rules govern every nation, and only three.

First, the choice is made once. Each descendant elects a nation during an open founding period; then the rolls close, as tribal rolls close. Choice is total at the founding; stability is total afterward. Each nation sets its own citizenship rules for children born after the closing. And because the choice is made before any rights are allocated, and is driven by identity rather than yield, no one can join a nation for its winnings.

Second, outcomes are determined entirely by free negotiation, nation to nation and nation to state. The program guarantees every nation a fair seat at the table. It guarantees no nation any particular result from it. A nation that governs and negotiates well may secure more for its citizens than one that does not. That is not a defect. That is what self-determination means.

Third, everything else belongs to the nations. Each writes its own constitution and chooses its own form of representation. We destroyed their institutions once. We will not commit the softer arrogance of drafting the replacements. Self-government is not the method of this policy. It is the point.

The nations will convene a coordinating assembly: the General Assembly of African American Republican Tribal Nations. Its initials are GAAARTN, and they are pronounced as one word: the Garden.

The name is the design. A garden is not a field of one crop. It is many beds of distinct flourishing in one shared ground, each community rooted where it grows best.

The Assembly binds no member and holds power over no nation. It exists so that equals may reach agreement freely, and its standing procedure is simple enough to state in full. The Garden publishes every monopoly that federal and state partners have opened. Each nation submits a sealed, ranked list of the monopolies it seeks. No nation sees another's hand. Uncontested first choices initiate negotiations between those nations and the government. Where nations seek the same industry monopoly in the same district, they first negotiate directly, by whatever method they agree on, with the Garden as facilitator and nothing more, then begin negotiations with the government when they have agreed on which districts each will negotiate for.

Mark the effect of this design. The nations arrive at the government's table having already settled among themselves who seeks what — and the government, seeing the whole pool at once, can pace its grants so that nothing given early exhausts what must remain for the states and nations that come later.

The federal government's part is brief by design. It opens categories by authorizing restorations — selectively and conditionally lifting each prohibition solely for a compacted nation, within a compacted geography, and for no one else, and it certifies the resulting compacts. It acquires no new discretionary power. It builds no new bureaucracy. It steps back when its work is done. The states participate voluntarily, industry by industry, district by district, exactly as Connecticut chose to. The monopolies bring in private capital; the compacts bind that capital to law; the nations put it to work.

Now the cost, stated exactly. The liberties themselves cost nothing to return; they are restorations, not payments. The Assembly runs on little more than a website, which the nations can operate themselves. The single public outlay is the Founding Census: an archival project, commissioned from historians of Africa and the Atlantic trade, to complete and verify the Founding Roster of Historic Nations, so that every founder chooses from a roll built on evidence.

Even that outlay is an advance, not an expense. A revolving Founding Fund fronts the cost. Once a nation's enterprises begin to earn, that nation remits a small fixed percentage until the advance is repaid with a fixed return, and the levy then expires permanently. Nothing is owed before a nation benefits. Nothing is owed after the fund is whole. Public money appears in this program exactly once. It goes out by rule, and it comes home by rule.

This program will face courts, and it is built to honor the parameters of justice as the Supreme Court has clarified them.

Citizenship freely chosen in a chartered nation is a political status, not a racial classification. That is the footing tribal membership has held in American law for two centuries, and the Supreme Court affirmed it in Morton v. Mancari in 1974. Because this program never asks anyone's race — not at founding, not at enrollment, not ever — it is not in conflict with the rulings against racial preferences, including Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard in 2023. That is not a technicality. It is the keystone. A program that encodes systematic racism into the architecture of American law and institutions, whether it’s racism in its ugliest form or the softer racism of racial preference mandates, is ultimately incompatible with American values and cannot last. A program of freely chosen political status is durable across administrations, across courts, and across the political spectrum, because it rests on the same ground the tribal compacts have stood on for generations, but more generally, because it is lawful, good and right.

Some will ask whether "republics" is the right word for the nations being restored. These nations are indeed small r republican oldest sense of the word: a republic is a government whose authorities answer to their people. Many of the destroyed nations governed by council, by assembly, and by law that answered to the governed, and every one of them tended its people’s interests incomparably better than Americasleaders represented those same people once enslaved here. To insist otherwise is to hold that America in 1850 was a republic while it kept millions in bondage, but that an Igbo village governing itself by open assembly was not. "Republics" is the truer word, but where statute requires a more legally precise term, "sovereign nations" will serve. Modern scholarship has restored to us the names and laws of the nations slavery destroyed — and with them, the chance to repair at last the stolen inheritance of wealth, of nation, and of pride in one's ancestors.

There have been piecemeal efforts here and there – a few received 40 acres and a mule, a typical inheritance that a son might receive from a father in that time — more recently, some have benefited by racial preference mandates and racial quotas — but the vast majority of American descendants of slavery have not received any benefit or restitution whatsoever – no special placement in hiring decisions – no restitution for the stolen inheritance of their ancestors’ wealth, nation and history. A history that, in many cases, will ring kindred and true in the hearts of many Americans.

Five centuries before Philadelphia, in the highlands of the Manden, a people broke a tyrant of their own. He is remembered as Soumaoro, the sorcerer-king of Sosso; the man who rose against him was Sundiata Keita. On the field at Kirina, around the year 1235, Sundiata shattered the tyrant's army — and then did the thing that turns a rebellion into a founding. He called the clans to a cleared plain named Kurukan Fuga, and there they proclaimed a charter: the human person made inviolable, the hungry to be fed, every clan given its voice, and the raiding of men to seize and sell them forevermore forbidden by law. A declaration of principles, spoken aloud at the birth of a nation, generations before our own. Their griots have carried its words from father to son for eight hundred years, and the world now honors it among the oldest constitutions of the human race.

More than four centuries after Sundiata, on the Gold Coast, the small Akan states lay under the heel of Denkyira, made to render tribute to a power not their own. A leader named Osei Tutu, with his counselor Okomfo Anokye, drew those scattered states into a single purpose, and in 1701, at Feyiase, they threw off Denkyira and stood up free. What they raised was not one throne over many peoples. It was a union of small nations, each keeping its own ruler and its own council, bound together beneath one law and one shared symbol.

So hear what this means, one and all.

If your ancestors came on the Mayflower or through Ellis Island, bold immigrants to a new nation like mine were several generations ago, then those brought here in chains were no strangers to our story; they were bearers of the same one. They came from nations that had risen against tyrants, written their charters, and raised republics of their own, exactly as the nation’s founding fathers would do on this soil.

And if you descend from those stolen citizens, then see the whole of what was taken: not your ancestors' freedom alone, but their nations' revolutions — Kirina and Feyiase standing beside Lexington and Yorktown, the charter of Kurukan Fuga beside the Declaration itself. One family of revolutions. One human hunger for liberty, breaking out again on every continent of the earth.

And let us be honest, in the way only the strong can afford. Some of those nations held slaves themselves, as very nearly every nation in human history has done, our own republic among them. That is not America's singular shame; it is humanity's common one. What is rare — what is almost without equal — is a nation whose founding words stood against the very wrong it had not yet overcome. Mali's charter forbade the seizing of men to sell them, and fell short of it. America proclaimed all men created equal, and fell short of it. That shared failure is not what divides these nations. It is one more bond between them: two peoples measured against a creed they themselves had raised, found wanting, and summoned by that same creed to rise and meet it. Such a creed, once spoken, never stops calling. It did not stop calling here. Let us all be humbled by the failures of humanity in slavery, be our ancient ancestry in Africa, Europe, or anywhere on earth - but let us be prouder still to become the nation that healed those wounds without sacrificing our principles — lifting those who’ve been wronged back up without pulling anyone else down.

Three objections will be raised against this program, and every one of them mistakes it for something it is not. Let us answer them now.

It is not a costly transfer, because there is nothing to transfer. No check is written to anyone. The one public outlay is an advance that repays itself and then extinguishes itself. Zero taxpayer cost.
It is not a preference, because a preference takes a seat that someone else earned, and this program is barred by its own admissibility test from touching any seat, contract, customer, queue, or market that any citizen holds or competes for. What it returns was taken from everyone long ago and has sat in the state's hands ever since and the state can give it freely by agreement.

And it is not a racial program, because it never asks anyone's race. It recognizes citizenship, freely chosen, rooted in nations that historically existed — the same political status American law has honored in tribal nations for two centuries.

Restoration, not preference. Liberty returned, not benefit conferred. A debtor paying its oldest debt out of what it already holds, with no bill sent to anyone. This is the check of which Doctor King spoke, and it is a check not paid in dollars, but in liberty — the freedom to develop an economic engine to replace the lost engine of inheritance taken from American descendants of slavery. It is a check drawn on a bank that cannot fail — stocked not with dollars, but with liberty

Picture the first years of this program.

In a Georgia town, a schoolteacher spends a winter with her grandmother's letters on one side of the kitchen table and the Founding Roster on the other. Her family's memory runs back eight generations and then goes silent, exactly as the trade intended. In the spring she does what no ancestor of hers was ever free to do. She elects her nation. That fall she stands in line to vote in its first election. She participates in the decision of which monopolies to pursue, in which districts and industries, and serves as an observer as they are negotiated with other nations, then with state and federal partners. She follows closely as her nation's flagship ventures raises funding from a private sector awash in capital. New enterprises are founded, funded and flourish, contributing to the local community and earning her nation standing and respect. In a matter of years she watches a council she helped seat — hers, answerable to her — gain the financing for a health clinic her county has needed for as long as she can remember, and the clinic opens its doors to everyone in the county.

Next door lives a man whose grandparents came through Ellis Island. Nothing was taken from him, and nothing was asked of him. His taxes did not rise. No market he works in gained a rival by government's hand. No opportunity his children were competing for was set aside for someone else. He watched a neighbor become a founder, his county gain a clinic, and his state gain a partner that has the economic engine to pay its own way.

Both of them were heard. Both of them own the outcome. That is what a remedy looks like when it passes both tests.

There is a day within reach of the people now living. On that day, the names of Kongo and Oyo, of Bundu and Futa Toro, of Owu and Dukandarra, and of the nations remembered now only by their rivers and coasts, are spoken again in America — as the names of living republics, governing themselves in open agreement and partnership with America and their host states — no longer lost to history.

On that day, the descendants of the people who carried the consent of the governed in their memory even as it was being set to parchment on this soil will practice it again, in nations of their own founding, under laws of their own writing. And the country that owed the debt will have finally paid it the only way a free people can: no one taxed, no one displaced, no one sorted by blood — and the wronged empowered by liberty and opportunity.

So let history record what The Garden is. It is not an apology for America. It is America doing the most American thing we can do – restoring an economic engine to Americans from whom it was taken, using proven market mechanisms and economic principles, backed by private capital freely invested rather than taxpayer dollars. When the founders met a wrong they could not live with, they did not curse the world, shrink from the challenge or look the other way; they chartered a better one. That is our method still: consent, enterprise, self-government, and debts honored to the letter. The Garden takes no one's field. It plants new fields. It raises republics by consent, endows them with liberty, and repays every public dollar with a return by fixed rule.

Two and a half centuries ago — two hundred fifty years, this very season — we declared that all men are created equal, and we pledged our sacred honor to the claim. Honor pledged is honor owed. The Garden is that pledge, kept: no one taxed, no one displaced, no one sorted by blood; the wronged made founders, and the whole country made more itself.

The check is written.

The ground stands fertile.

The seeds are in your hands.
